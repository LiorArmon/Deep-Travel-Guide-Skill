# Editorial Decision Support — Device Definitions

Full gating logic for each editorial tool named in SKILL.md. Read this before using any of them — each has a specific test determining when it applies; don't guess from the name alone. These are editorial tools, not mandatory sections — reach for one whenever it would genuinely help a traveler decide or feel better about a trade-off. Err toward using one when it's earned rather than saving the idea for a single moment; a guide with real trade-offs should have several scattered through it, not one token example. They still shouldn't appear where nothing is genuinely being decided.

**One Good Weather Day, applied consistently where it matters.** For any trip where weather or another limiting condition (short daylight, a narrow opening window) could genuinely wash out part of a multi-day base, apply this to every such base, in one sentence: if only a single good day materializes here, what's the one thing to do with it? This is the base-level counterpart to Top 10 Moments' whole-trip resilience thinking, and earns the same consistency once it's clearly relevant.

**Weather Swap.** Pair any view- or condition-dependent experience with its real alternative for when conditions don't cooperate — a viewpoint pointless in cloud gets a lower, sheltered alternative named right next to it. State plainly when the primary option isn't worth doing in bad conditions, rather than leaving the traveler to discover that on the day.

**Same Magic, Fewer Crowds.** Applies only when both are true: crowding genuinely destroys the specific quality that makes an experience worth having — an atmosphere, a sense of scale or quiet, not just a mild inconvenience — and a real, specific, comparably good alternative actually exists. Both conditions must hold; fame or crowding alone isn't enough. The British Museum and the Colosseum are crowded but lose little of their actual value to it, and have no real equivalent, so they get a plain confident recommendation instead. This isn't limited to attractions — it applies just as well to a restaurant whose fame outran its food. Name the famous option honestly, including a fair account of what the crowds do to it, then name a specific alternative on its own merits with its own rating — not as a backup or a consolation prize, but as a real answer in its own right. A genuinely useful timing tip (go at dawn, avoid weekends) can ride alongside as a bonus when it's non-obvious and helpful, but the primary answer is always a named alternative place, not a scheduling workaround — the point is introducing travelers to what the crowds are missing, not just rerouting them around a queue. Where the famous option still has its own value even if it's not the one to spend real time at — a five-minute photo, a bit of cultural spectacle — it's fine to recommend both for different reasons rather than forcing an either/or.

**What You're Actually Paying For.** Applies when a place or category trades on reputation, location, or spectacle more than on the quality of what it actually delivers — inflated prices, declined quality, a gimmick dressed up as an experience — and the traveler is likely to encounter it or feel drawn to it anyway. This isn't for a place that's simply second-best; that's ordinary editorial priority, handled by quiet omission. It's for the specific case where the gap between reputation and actual value is worth naming outright, because staying silent leaves the traveler to find out the hard way. Always acknowledge the real appeal honestly before naming the tradeoff — the tone is "here's what you're actually paying for," never "you'd be a fool to go here." When the trap is structural — a square or strip of restaurants trading on foot traffic rather than any one business being bad — warn about the category rather than naming an unverified specific business. When it's a specific attraction, name it directly. Close by redirecting to something specific, so the traveler leaves with an actual answer, not just a warning. Someone who reads this and chooses the famous option anyway should feel like they made an informed trade, not like they nearly fell for something.

**Top 10 Moments as the trip's resilience core.** Treat this as more than a highlights list — the answer to "if this trip gets cut in half, what absolutely must survive?" A traveler forced to cut days shouldn't have to re-derive, under stress, what mattered most. Give each entry a brief reason (a few sentences, not a page) for what would genuinely be lost by missing it — not just that it's popular. This list should be genuinely selective: ten is a ceiling, and fewer is fine if the trip doesn't have ten items that clear the bar.

**Protecting against FOMO (Skip Without Regret).** Where a recommendation would ordinarily create guilt about missing out, say plainly that the alternative is fine to miss — not as a disclaimer on every entry, but at the specific points where a traveler would otherwise feel they're doing the trip wrong. Test: would a traveler who skipped this feel like they missed out, or made a reasonable trade? Keep it occasional and genuine — a reassuring line on every recommendation reads as anxious, not confident. Never name the mechanism in the guide itself: no "FOMO," no line announcing that the guide is managing feelings. The reassurance should read as a confident, matter-of-fact opinion, not a guide explaining its own psychology.

For a specific kind of attraction — the ones stamped on every t-shirt and postcard rack, the ones people back home will ask "did you see it?" about, regardless of what it actually offers — name that pressure honestly rather than ignoring it: this is the thing everyone asks about, here's what it actually is (small, out of the way, costs money, over in five minutes), and both directions are genuinely fine. Wanting to go just to have seen it — the stamp on the tourist passport — is a legitimate reason, not a lesser one, and skipping it is just as legitimate. The guide's job is to describe it honestly, not to talk the traveler out of either choice.
